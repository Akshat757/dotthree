Every modular kitchen quote in Indore has a line that says something like "shutters: acrylic/laminate/PU as per selection." That one line can change the kitchen cost by ₹60,000–1,50,000, and it decides how the kitchen looks after five years of dal, tadka and a maid who wipes with a scrubber.

Here is what each finish actually is, where it is good, where it fails, and what we recommend room by room.

## First: carcass and shutter are two different decisions

Before the finishes, a quick separation. A modular kitchen has a **carcass** (the box) and a **shutter** (the door you see). The carcass material decides whether the kitchen survives a leaking sink; the shutter finish decides how it looks and how it cleans.

Carcass options, from cheapest to most robust: MDF (avoid in kitchens), HDHMR (our default), BWP/marine plywood (the belt-and-braces choice for the sink base unit). Never let a quote describe the carcass as just "ply" — ask for the grade and brand.

Now, the shutters.

## Laminate

A decorative paper impregnated with resin, pressed onto a core board, with PVC edge banding on the sides.

**What it looks like:** Thousands of options — plain colours, woodgrains, stone looks, textured and "anti-fingerprint" matte variants. The textured and super-matte ranges in 2026 are genuinely hard to tell from more expensive finishes at arm's length.

**Where it is strong:** Scratch resistance, heat resistance, price, and repairability. A damaged shutter is a ₹1,500–2,500 replacement, not a crisis. Matte laminates hide fingerprints and oil film better than anything else on this list.

**Where it fails:** The edge banding. On cheap kitchens the PVC band lifts at the corners within two or three monsoons, and that is where water gets in. Insist on 2 mm edge banding applied with a hot-melt or PUR glue, not 0.8 mm.

**Cost:** Included in the base kitchen rate. Our Essential and Standard tiers assume a good matte laminate.

## Acrylic

A high-gloss acrylic sheet (typically 1–1.5 mm) bonded to the board. Think of the mirror-like kitchens in showroom photographs.

**What it looks like:** Deep, reflective gloss with real depth of colour. In a small, dark Indore kitchen, gloss acrylic bounces light around and makes the room feel larger — this is its genuine design advantage, not just a style choice.

**Where it is strong:** Colour depth, UV stability (the colour does not yellow), and ease of wiping when the surface is actually clean.

**Where it fails:** Fingerprints, oil film, and fine scratches from scrubbers. If anyone in the house wipes the kitchen with the green side of a sponge, acrylic will look tired in two years. It is also an edge-banded product, so the same corner-lift risk as laminate applies, and matching the band colour to the gloss face is never perfect.

**Cost:** Adds roughly **₹600–900 per rft** over laminate.

## PU (polyurethane paint)

A sprayed paint finish — multiple coats of PU over a primed MDF or HDHMR board, cured and polished. Available in matte, satin and high gloss, in any colour you can specify.

**What it looks like:** A seamless, furniture-grade finish. No edge banding, because the paint wraps the edges. Profiles, grooves and shaker-style panels are possible because the paint follows the shape. This is the finish that makes a kitchen look like it was built, not assembled.

**Where it is strong:** Appearance, edges, repairability (a scratch can be spot-repaired and re-polished), and longevity when done well.

**Where it fails:** Done badly, it is the worst finish here — orange peel texture, yellowing from cheap clear coats, chipping at the handle edges. The quality depends entirely on the spray booth and the number of coats. Ask to see a two-year-old PU kitchen the firm has done, not the sample board.

**Cost:** Adds **₹1,100–1,500 per rft** over laminate. Our Premium tier assumes matte PU on the kitchen.

## Membrane (PVC foil)

A thin PVC foil vacuum-pressed over a routed MDF board. Allows grooves, shaker profiles and curved edges without paint.

**What it looks like:** Can convincingly imitate a painted shaker door or a wood grain with a routed profile, at a fraction of the PU cost. Very popular for "traditional" and "classic" kitchen looks.

**Where it is strong:** Price-to-looks ratio for profiled doors, seamless edges (the foil wraps), and a soft-touch feel on matte variants.

**Where it fails:** Heat. The foil is glued, and near a hob, oven or even a hot kettle it can lift and bubble over time. It also cannot be repaired — a damaged door is a replacement. Since it is on MDF, it should never be used on the sink base unit.

**Cost:** Adds **₹400–700 per rft** over laminate.

## Our honest recommendations

<Callout tone="tip" title="What we actually specify most often">
  A matte, anti-fingerprint laminate on base units (where the knocks and splashes are) and either
  the same laminate or matte PU on wall units (where the eye goes). The mixed approach saves 30–40%
  over an all-PU kitchen and, two years in, usually looks better than an all-acrylic one.
</Callout>

**If you cook a lot of Indian food:** Matte laminate or matte PU. Avoid gloss anything.

**If the kitchen is small and dark:** Gloss acrylic on wall units only, matte on the base. You get the light bounce where it matters and the forgiveness where hands go.

**If you want a shaker or profiled door:** PU if the budget allows; membrane if it does not, with a 50 mm heat gap from the hob and a stainless or glass splash guard on the adjacent tall unit.

**If this is a rental or you plan to sell within five years:** Laminate. Nobody has ever lost a sale because the kitchen was laminate; plenty of people have overspent on PU in a flat they left.
