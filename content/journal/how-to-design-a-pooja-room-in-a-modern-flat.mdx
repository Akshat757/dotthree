In most Indore flats, the pooja room is a decision the builder made for you — a 2 ft by 3 ft niche with a plywood shelf, somewhere near the kitchen. In others, there is no provision at all, and the mandir ends up on a wardrobe shelf in the parents' room.

Neither has to be the case. A good pooja space in a modern flat is small, considered, and quietly the most-loved part of the house. Here is how we design one.

## Start with placement

If you are planning the layout from scratch — a new flat before possession, or a full renovation — placement comes first.

**The vastu preference** is the north-east (ishan) corner, with the idols facing west so that you face east while praying. The second preference is the east or north wall. Most clients who ask for vastu alignment want this, and it is usually achievable in a living room or a dining area.

**The practical considerations** matter as much:

- Not in a bedroom, if at all avoidable. Guests and daily life clash.
- Not sharing a wall with a bathroom or directly below one in a duplex.
- Not under a staircase or in a passage where people walk past with their backs to it.
- Near natural light if possible — morning light on the mandir is the point of an east-facing position.
- With a wall behind it that can take a small amount of soot and heat.

In a typical 2BHK, the compromise we most often reach is a **wall-mounted unit on the east or north wall of the living or dining area**, 24–36 in wide, with a drop ceiling above it and a concealed light. It reads as part of the room's design, not a cupboard.

In 3BHK and larger flats, a **dedicated 3 ft × 4 ft room or alcove** is usually possible by borrowing from a passage or a utility corner.

## Size it for how you actually pray

The most common mistake is making the unit for the idols rather than for the ritual. Measure what happens during a morning pooja:

- **Idols and frames**: a platform 18–24 in wide usually holds a family's idols with space to move them for cleaning. Depth 12–14 in.
- **Diya and agarbatti**: a separate ledge or the front edge of the platform, in a heat-safe material, at least 6 in in front of anything that can scorch.
- **Thali, water, flowers, prasad**: a working surface 10–12 in deep at 30–34 in height. This is why most units have a lower slab.
- **Books, extra lamps, matches, cloth**: two drawers or a shutter below, 14–18 in tall.
- **Bell**: a hook at 5 ft 6 in to 6 ft on the unit or ceiling.

For a seated pooja, you need 30 in of clear floor in front of the unit. For a standing one, 24 in.

## Materials that survive a diya

<Callout tone="warning" title="Laminate and the diya do not get along">
  We are called at least once a month to replace a scorched laminate shelf under a diya. Whatever
  the unit is made of, the surface the diya sits on must be stone — a 20 mm marble or granite slab,
  or a tile. Backlit onyx looks beautiful and also works.
</Callout>

**Carcass:** BWP plywood. The unit sees water daily — from the kalash, the flowers, the wiping.

**Face:** Teak veneer or a good teak-finish laminate on the vertical surfaces. Natural veneer with a PU finish ages beautifully; it is the one place in a flat where we push clients toward veneer over laminate because the unit is small and the upgrade is ₹8,000–15,000.

**Back wall:** The three materials that work are a CNC-cut jaali panel (MDF or WPC, painted or veneered) with a light behind it; a thin natural stone (Dholpur, white marble, or a textured sandstone); or a simple painted wall in a deep colour — terracotta, saffron, indigo — with a brass element. Wallpaper near a diya is a bad idea.

**Floor:** Marble or a marble-look tile in the walk-in format. In the alcove format, a marble slab inset into the existing floor.

**Doors:** Jaali shutters in the same veneer, or a pair of brass-inlay doors if the budget allows. Glass doors look modern but fog with incense; if you want glass, make it fluted and leave a gap at the top.

## Lighting: three layers, two switches

1. **Backlight** behind the jaali or around the back panel edge — a warm 2700K LED strip. This is the light that stays on in the evening.
2. **Downlight** from a concealed cove or a single 7 W spot in the drop above the unit, aimed at the idols.
3. **Niche or diya light** — a small 1 W warm light on the lower ledge for the morning routine.

Two switches: one for the backlight (on from dusk), one for the rest. Avoid cool white anywhere in a pooja room; it flattens brass and marble.

## Ventilation — the thing everyone forgets

Incense and camphor smoke stain a ceiling within a year. In a walk-in or alcove pooja room, provide a 4 in exhaust fan or at minimum a louvred panel at the top of the unit. In a wall-mounted unit, the drop ceiling above it should have 2–3 in of clearance to the slab with a gap at the back so smoke does not pool.

## Making it feel modern

A pooja room does not have to be carved, gold and red to be traditional. The approach we use for contemporary flats:

- **One traditional material, one modern.** Teak veneer with a plain white marble back. Or a CNC jaali in a flat matte paint against a textured wall.
- **A single warm colour.** Terracotta, mustard or a deep green on one surface, everything else neutral.
- **Brass as the accent** — the bell, the diya stand, a thin inlay line — rather than carved gilt.
- **Hide the storage.** Push-to-open drawers in the same finish so the unit reads as a single piece.
- **Repeat a detail from the room** — the same fluted panel as the TV wall, or the same stone as the dining table.
