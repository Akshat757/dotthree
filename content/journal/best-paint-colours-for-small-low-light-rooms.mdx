The brief is always the same: "The room is small and does not get much light, so we want it bright white." And the result is nearly always a room that feels like a waiting area — flat, slightly grey, and somehow smaller than before.

White needs light to be white. In a north-facing bedroom in a flat where the neighbouring tower blocks the afternoon sun, white walls reflect whatever little light there is back as a dull grey. The room does not feel bigger. It feels unfinished.

Here is what actually works.

## The one number to learn: LRV

**Light Reflectance Value** is the percentage of light a colour bounces back. Pure white is around 90; black is around 5. Every paint company publishes it on the swatch or in the fan deck.

For a small, low-light room the useful range is **LRV 60–82**. High enough to keep the room lifted, low enough that the colour has some warmth and body. Below 60 the room gets cosy and intentional, which is a valid choice for a bedroom — but it is a choice, not a default.

The reason this matters more than the colour name: two "off-whites" from the same brand can differ by 15 LRV points and behave like different colours on a dim wall.

## Warm beats cool in low light

Low daylight in Indore flats is usually bluish (north-facing, or reflected off a neighbouring building). A cool grey or a blue-white on top of that goes clinical. Warm undertones — a touch of yellow, pink or red in the neutral — counteract it.

Specifically:

- **Warm off-whites** (LRV 75–82): the closest thing to a safe default. Think the colour of unbleached cotton, not a hospital wall.
- **Greige and mushroom** (LRV 55–68): warm grey-beige. Flatters wood furniture, hides scuffs, looks expensive under warm artificial light.
- **Clay, terracotta-tint and blush** (LRV 50–65): the pinks and oranges that look alarming on a swatch and beautiful on a whole wall in dim light. Our most-complimented bedroom colours.
- **Sage and olive-grey** (LRV 40–58): the one cool-ish family that works, because the yellow in the green warms it. Good with cane, oak and brass.

## Mid-tones: the counter-intuitive move

For a genuinely dim room — a bedroom where the light is on all day — a **mid-tone on all four walls and the ceiling** often works better than any light colour. A deep sage, an inky teal, a warm terracotta at LRV 20–35. The room stops trying to be bright and becomes enveloping instead. Corners disappear, the walls recede, and with two warm lamps the effect is a reading nook rather than a cell.

This is the hardest sell and the one clients are most grateful for after. If you are nervous, do it in the smallest room first — a study or a guest room.

## Finish matters as much as colour

- **Matte** on walls and ceilings in small rooms. It hides the putty imperfections that low light otherwise accentuates with a raking shadow, and it lets the colour read evenly.
- **Avoid high sheen**. Satin and silk finishes pick up every window and lamp as a hot spot, which fragments a small wall.
- **The exception:** a washable matte or an eggshell in a kids' room and the passage.

## The ceiling trick

Paint the ceiling the wall colour, or the wall colour lightened by about 25%. The hard horizon line between a coloured wall and a white ceiling draws the eye to how low the ceiling is. Removing it makes the room read taller. In a room with a false ceiling cove, keep the inner panel the same as the walls and let the cove light do the lifting.

## Test it under your own light

<Callout tone="tip" title="The two-board test">
  Buy a 200 ml sample tin. Paint two A3 boards of foam core, two coats. Tape one to the dimmest wall
  and one next to the window. Look at them at 8 am, 2 pm and 9 pm with the lamps on. If you like the
  dim-wall board at 9 pm, buy the paint. The window board will always look better; it is the dim one
  that you will live with.
</Callout>

Never choose from a 2 in swatch, a screen, or the shop's lighting. A colour that is fine on a chip is often two shades stronger on a 100 sq ft wall.

## Lighting is half of the colour

A low-light room cannot be fixed by paint alone. The light you add changes the colour you see:

- **2700–3000K warm white** everywhere in bedrooms. Cooler than that and every warm neutral above goes muddy.
- **Two sources minimum**, both low: a bedside lamp and a floor or wall light. Avoid relying on the one ceiling fitting, which lights the floor and leaves the walls dark.
- **Wash the wall** — an uplight or a cove that grazes the wall surface makes the room feel lit even when the lux level is low.
- **Mirror opposite the window**, not opposite the door. It doubles the brightest surface in the room.
