"Forty-five days" is the interior industry's most repeated promise. It is not a lie, exactly — it is the length of the site-work phase, in an empty flat, with the material already made, and no changes. Measured from the day you sign to the day you move in, a 2BHK takes closer to nine or ten weeks, and a 3BHK with civil work closer to fourteen.

Here is where the days go.

The overlap is the key. Factory production runs while the site crew does services and the ceiling. A firm that starts production only after the ceiling is done adds three weeks to everything.

## Week by week: a 2BHK, empty flat

**Weeks 1–3: Design.**
Measured survey, layout options, one round of revision, 3D views for the kitchen and living, material and shade selection, working drawings. This phase ends with a signature on every drawing. Every day it slips, every subsequent date slips.

**Week 3 (end): Material order and production start.**
Plywood, laminates and hardware ordered. The factory's clock starts. This is also when the final BOQ and payment milestone fall.

**Week 4: Services.**
Electrician marks and chases conduits, pulls wires, fixes boxes. Plumber adjusts points for the kitchen sink, RO, and washing machine. AC piping and drain run before the ceiling closes. Core drilling for the chimney. This week is loud.

**Week 5: Ceiling frame and board.**
GI frame, gypsum board, jointing. Fan boxes and spotlight cut-outs. Electrician returns to drop wires through the ceiling.

**Week 6: Putty and first coat.**
Two coats of putty on walls and ceiling, sanding, primer, first coat of emulsion. This is the dustiest week.

**Week 6 (end): Material arrives on site.**
Modular units land as flat-packed carcasses and shutters. This is the "day one" of the 45-day promise — notice it is already week six.

**Weeks 7–8: Installation.**
Kitchen carcasses, wall units, tall unit. Wardrobe carcasses in both bedrooms. Countertop templating happens once base units are fixed; the granite or quartz comes back in 4–6 days. TV unit, shoe unit, pooja unit. Shutters and hardware go on last.

**Week 9: Finishing.**
Final coat of paint, after all the carpentry dust. Light fittings, switch plates, mirrors, curtain rods. Loose furniture delivery. Snag walk-through and list.

**Week 10: Snag closure and handover.**
Two to four days of touch-ups — a shutter alignment, a paint scuff, a soft-close that is not. Deep clean. Walk-through with the drawings. Keys.

## The five things that add weeks

1. **Late decisions.** Every "we'll finalise the laminate next week" during production is a week added. The shade for the kitchen shutters is needed on the day production starts, not when the carcasses arrive.
2. **Changes after sign-off.** Moving a wardrobe by 6 in after the drawings are in the factory means a re-cut and a re-order. Allow one revision round in design and then stop.
3. **Civil surprises.** Tiles that come up with the plaster, a leaking joint in the flat above, a beam where the drawing said none. Budget a week's contingency on any project with civil work.
4. **Society and access.** Lift permissions, working-hour restrictions, a festival week with no labour. Diwali, Holi and the wedding season in November–February each cost a week.
5. **Countertop lead time.** Quartz from a national brand is often 10–14 days. Order it the day base units are fixed, not after the shutters.

## Occupied homes and civil work

Both change the picture. An occupied home runs rooms sequentially instead of in parallel — see [our occupied-home checklist](/journal/a-realistic-renovation-checklist-for-an-occupied-home/) — and adds 25–35%. Civil work adds a two-to-three-week phase before the ceiling can start and introduces the only genuinely unpredictable element in the schedule.

For a new flat before possession, the schedule above holds, and [preparing the flat properly](/journal/how-to-prepare-your-new-flat-before-the-interior-team-arrives/) is what makes the services week go smoothly.
